⏱️ Timeouts, Retries & Backoff with Jitter

Marc Brooker · Amazon Builders' Library · 2019 · Study guide for #1
🟢 Level 1 ⏱️ Original: 20 min ⏱️ Guide: ~20 min + 25 min lab 🔗 Read the original
🔗 #2 Idempotent APIs: what makes a retry safe 🔗 #25 Cascading Failures: retries during overload 🔗 #24 Tail at Scale: hedged requests 🔗 #3 Caching: jittered TTLs stop stampedes 🔗 #20 End-to-End: the caller owns correctness

⚡ TL;DR

🧩 The problem: your service calls other services over the network (an API, a DB, Redis). Sometimes they're slow, or fail for a moment. How should your code react?

⏱️ Timeout (max wait): never wait forever. Give every call a time limit, based on how slow that service normally is.

🔁 Retry (try again): only for temporary errors, only when repeating is safe, only a few times, in one place. Every retry is extra load on a service that may already be struggling.

🎲 Backoff + jitter (wait longer, plus a random bit): so 1,000 clients that failed together don't all try again at the same instant.

👉 In one line: don't wait forever, don't retry blindly, and never retry in sync.

🗺️ The whole article as one flow, what your code does on every remote call:

📞 Call the service, with a timeout (e.g. 800 ms)
↓ it failed or timed out
🤔 Worth retrying? Temporary error (503, timeout)? Safe to repeat? Attempts left? Retry budget left?
↓ yes
⏳ Wait: backoff (100, 200, 400 ms…) × random jitter, then call again
↓ no
🛑 Fail fast: return the error, show a fallback, or queue it for later

🔤 Terms first

Timeout
The longest a client will wait for a call before giving up.
Retry
Sending the same request again after a failure.
Backoff
Waiting longer between each retry. Exponential: 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms… (doubles each time), usually with a cap (a maximum wait).
Jitter
Adding randomness to that wait so clients don't retry at the same moment.
Transient failure
A failure that goes away on its own after a short time (a blip).
Latency
How long one call takes, from sending the request to getting the full response.
p50, p99, p99.9
Line up 1,000 calls from fastest to slowest. p50 is the 500th call's time (a typical call). p99 is the 990th: only 10 calls were slower. p99.9 is the 999th: only 1 call was slower. Full explainer with a chart in Walkthrough §2.
Idempotent
Safe to repeat: doing it twice has the same effect as once. Reading a user: yes. Charging a card: no, unless the API takes an idempotency key.
Thundering herd
Many clients hitting a server at the same moment, often right after it recovers.
Retry budget
A cap on retries as a share of normal traffic (say at most 10%), often built as a token bucket: each retry spends a token, successes slowly earn tokens back.

🧠 Mental model

🍽️ A slammed restaurant kitchen. If every waiter re-shouts their order every 5 seconds, the kitchen gets slower, not faster. If every waiter waits exactly 30 seconds and then shouts together, it's still a wall of noise, just later. What works: each waiter waits a bit longer each time and picks a slightly random moment. Orders arrive spread out, and the kitchen catches up.

📖 Walkthrough

1. Failures happen

What it says: slow requests hold resources (memory, threads, connections, ports). Many failures are partial (some requests fail) or transient (fail briefly, then work). Traffic also arrives in bursts.

What it means for you: a hung call isn't "just slow". In Node, each in-flight request holds a socket, a DB pool slot and memory for its closures. Enough of them and your healthy service falls over because of someone else's slow one.

2. Timeouts

What it says: set a connection timeout (max wait to open a connection) and a request timeout (max wait for the answer) on every remote call. Pick the value from the downstream service's latency data: choose an acceptable false-timeout rate, like 0.1%, and use the matching percentile, p99.9. Then it lists pitfalls and a 20 ms story. All of these are unpacked below.

📊 First, percentiles (p50, p99, p99.9), because the whole section depends on them.

🏃 Picture 1,000 runners in a race. Sort them by finish time. p50 is the time of runner #500: half finished faster. p99 is runner #990: only 10 people were slower. p99.9 is runner #999: only 1 person was slower.

📞 Now the runners are 1,000 calls to an API. "p99.9 = 1,004 ms" means: 999 of 1,000 calls finished within 1,004 ms, and 1 took longer.

🤔 Why not just use the average? Because most calls are fast and a few are very slow. The average hides those few, but your users still hit them. If one page load makes 100 backend calls, the chance that at least one of them is slower than p99 is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. So the "rare" slow tail shows up on most page loads.

📏 Now the article's words, one at a time:

  • ❌ False timeout: a call that would have succeeded, but your timeout gave up first. The service was fine, just slower than your limit. You still treat it as a failure, and probably retry, adding load.
  • 🎯 Acceptable false-timeout rate: how many of those you're willing to cause. "0.1%" means "I'm OK cutting off 1 healthy call in 1,000".
  • 🔗 Matching percentile: if you accept cutting off the slowest 0.1%, set the timeout to the time that the other 99.9% beat. That's exactly p99.9.

⚠️ The article's pitfalls, in plain words:

  • 🌍 Far-away clients. Latency measured inside one AWS region doesn't include a long trip over the internet. For those clients, add the worst-case network time on top.
  • 📏 Very tight latency. If p50 is 10 ms and p99.9 is 12 ms, a 12 ms timeout trips on any small hiccup, like a GC pause. Add padding.
  • 🕳️ Timeouts that don't cover the whole call. A remote call has several steps, and some timeout settings only cover the last ones:
    🔎 DNS lookup (name → IP) → 🔌 TCP connect → 🔐 TLS handshake (agree on encryption) → 📤 send request → ⚙️ server works → 📥 read response
    If your timeout only starts at 📤, a stuck DNS lookup or handshake can hang far longer than your "timeout". The article's advice: use a well-tested client whose timeout covers everything. In Node, AbortSignal.timeout() on fetch covers the whole call.

🏭 The paper's 20 ms story, step by step

  1. A service at Amazon called another service with a ~20 ms timeout. Normal calls took a few ms, so 20 ms looked generous.
  2. That timeout also covered opening a secure connection (the TLS handshake: a few round trips to agree on encryption keys).
  3. Normally that cost is invisible. Clients keep connections open and reuse them (keep-alive, a connection pool), so most calls skip the handshake.
  4. After every deploy, a new server starts with zero open connections. Its first calls must do the handshake, which took longer than 20 ms on its own. Result: a small burst of timeouts right after each deploy, only on new servers.
  5. First fix: a longer timeout just for setting up new connections. Better fix: open the connections when the process starts, before it takes traffic (warm-up).

Bar lengths are illustrative; the article only says the handshake took longer than 20 ms.

🛠️ In NestJS terms: warm your HTTP and DB connection pools in onApplicationBootstrap(), and only report the pod ready (the readiness probe) after that. The lesson: a timeout covers more than you think, and the first call on a fresh server is not a normal call.

What it means for you: if the payment API's p99.9 is 350 ms, a ~400 ms timeout cuts off about 1 in 1,000 calls that would have succeeded, and you never hang for 30 seconds. Too high and the timeout protects nothing. Too low and you create retries, and possibly an outage, yourself.

3. Retries and backoff

What it says: retries are selfish: a retry spends the server's capacity to raise your chance of success. That's fine when failures are rare and random. When failures come from overload, retries add even more load and slow recovery. So back off exponentially with a cap, and limit the number of retries. Then it lists four complications.

3a. 📚 Retries multiply across layers

Real requests pass through a chain of services. If every layer retries on its own, the retries multiply. Start small, with 2 layers that each try up to 3 times, while the database is down:

  1. The API calls the Order service. It fails, so the API tries again, 3 times in total.
  2. Each of those 3 calls reaches the Order service, which calls the database. It fails, so the Order service also tries 3 times.
  3. The database gets 3 × 3 = 9 calls for one click. With a third layer it's 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. The article's 5 layers: 3⁵ = 243 calls.

So a database that's struggling sees its load jump up to 243× exactly when it can least handle it. The article's fix: retry at one point in the stack, and let every other layer fail fast.

3b. 🪣 Cap retries with a token bucket

Picture a jar of 10 tokens. Each retry takes a token; each success slowly puts some back. While there are tokens, retry freely. When the jar is empty, the article's version drops to retrying at a slow, fixed rate. So during a real outage, retries stay a small share of traffic instead of multiplying it. Amazon prefers this to circuit breakers (a switch that blocks all calls after too many failures), whose on/off behavior is hard to test and slow to recover. The AWS SDK added this in 2016.

3c. 🔑 Only retry what's safe to repeat

  • ✅ GET /users/42 twice: you read the same user twice. Harmless. Reads are usually safe.
  • ❌ POST /orders twice: possibly two orders. And a timeout doesn't mean it failed. The first order may have been created; you just didn't hear back.
  • 🔑 The fix is an idempotency key: the client sends a unique id (a UUID) with the create, the server remembers "already handled this id" and returns the first result instead of creating again. EC2's RunInstances takes one; so does Stripe. That's paper #2.

3d. 🚦 Know which errors to retry

The test is simple: could the same request succeed if sent again in a moment? HTTP status codes tell you most of it. 4xx means "your request is wrong", so sending the same request again gets the same answer. 5xx means "the server had a problem", which may clear up.

Status / errorMeansRetry?
400, 422Bad input❌ No. Fix the request.
401Not logged in / token expired❌ No. Refresh the token once, then it's a new request.
403Not allowed❌ No.
404Not found❌ Usually not. ⚠️ Exception below.
409Conflict (e.g. version mismatch)❌ Not blindly. Re-read, then decide.

⚠️ Eventual consistency blurs the line (the article's point). Many databases copy writes to read replicas a moment later. Create a user, read it back from a replica 5 ms later, and you can get a 404 that turns into a 200 soon after. So for "read right after write" flows, a short retry on 404 can be correct. Know your data store.

4. Jitter

What it says: backoff alone keeps retries correlated: everyone who failed together retries together. So add randomness. And not only to retries: timers, cron jobs and periodic work cause spikes too. Amazon uses deterministic jitter for scheduled work, and found that spreading out clients that fired every minute on the minute let them do the same work with less capacity.

🐑 Why backoff alone isn't enough. Say 100 clients all fail at the same moment, t = 0.

  • Backoff, no jitter: every client computes the same wait. All of them wait 100 ms and retry together at t = 100. They collide, so all wait 200 ms and retry together at t = 300, then t = 700… They move as one herd, so every retry is a spike. Backoff only made the spikes rarer.
  • Backoff + full jitter: each client waits a random time between 0 and the backoff. On the first retry, one client waits 13 ms, another 78 ms, another 41 ms. The herd breaks up into a steady trickle the server can handle.
const ceiling = Math.min(capMs, baseMs * 2 ** attempt); // 100, 200, 400… up to the cap

const noJitter = ceiling;                    // everyone waits exactly the same
const fullJitter = Math.random() * ceiling;  // everyone waits a different 0…ceiling

📖 How to read it. Top: each row is one client, and each dot is a moment it retried. Across is time since the failure. Bottom: how many retries hit the server in each 10 ms window. A tall bar is a spike.

👀 What to look for. No jitter: the dots stack into 5 straight columns, so the server gets 5 spikes of 20 retries with nothing in between. Full jitter: the dots scatter, and the bottom bars stay low. It's the same number of retries, just spread out. That's the whole point of jitter.

⏰ Jitter for scheduled work, too. A 0 0 * * * cron (midnight every night) running on 20 pods hits your database at the same second. Amazon saw the same with clients that fired "every minute, on the minute". Their per-minute graphs looked fine, because the average over 60 seconds was low. Only per-second graphs showed the spike in the first second of each minute. Spreading those clients across the minute let them run the same work on fewer servers.

🎯 Deterministic jitter: for scheduled jobs, Amazon does not pick a new random offset each run. Each host gets the same offset every time, computed from something stable like its hostname. Why? If load spikes at 00:03:17 every night, you can trace it to the one host that always runs then. With fresh randomness, the spike would move around and be much harder to debug.

What it means for you: give each pod its own fixed offset of a few minutes for nightly jobs (code in At work), and use random jitter for retries and reconnects.

5. Conclusion

What it says: retry only when the dependency looks healthy, and stop when retries aren't improving availability.

What it means for you: retries are for blips, not outages. During an outage, failing fast is the kind thing to do.

⚖️ Tradeoffs

ChoiceYou gainYou give up
Short timeoutResources freed fast, quick failureFalse timeouts on slow-but-fine calls; more retries
Long timeoutFewer false failuresSockets and pool slots held while waiting; slow UX
More retriesHigher success on random blipsLoad amplification in overload; longer tail latency
Retry only at the top layerNo multiplicationRedoes work lower layers already did
Random jitterSpreads loadHarder to reproduce timing bugs (hence deterministic jitter for crons)

If assumptions change: the advice assumes failures are mostly transient. If a dependency is hard down, every retry is waste. If clients are not yours (mobile apps, partners), you can't enforce jitter, so the server must protect itself with throttling and load shedding (#25).

🌱 Then vs now

  • ✅ Still current practice. AWS SDKs ship retry modes with backoff, jitter and a retry token bucket. gRPC supports retry policies with throttling, and Envoy supports retry budgets.
  • 🤔 Circuit breakers: the article's skepticism is Amazon's view, not a law. Netflix popularized them with Hystrix (now in maintenance mode) and many teams still use them. 🧠 My take: retry budgets plus server-side load shedding degrade more gracefully than an on/off breaker.
  • ➕ Missing from the article: deadline propagation. Pass the remaining time budget down the call chain (gRPC deadlines do this), so lower layers stop working on requests the caller already gave up on. Covered in #25.
  • 🗣️ What to say today: "timeouts from latency percentiles, retries in one layer, exponential backoff with full jitter, a retry budget, idempotency keys for writes, and deadlines propagated down the stack."

🛠️ At work

Examples are NestJS + TypeScript on Node 20+. The helpers are plain TypeScript, so they work in any Node service.

1. A timeout on every call

Many Node clients have no timeout, or a very long one, by default. Worth checking in your versions: axios (what Nest's HttpModule uses) defaults to no timeout, and a pg pool waits forever for a free connection unless you set connectionTimeoutMillis.

// app.module.ts: one place to set it for every HttpService call
@Module({
  imports: [
    HttpModule.register({
      timeout: 800, // ms; axios default is 0 = wait forever
    }),
  ],
})
export class AppModule {}
// Built-in fetch: pass a signal that aborts after 800 ms
const res = await fetch(url, {
  signal: AbortSignal.timeout(800),
});

2. Decide what's retryable (the status-code table, as code)

// retry.ts
export class HttpError extends Error {
  constructor(readonly status: number) {
    super(`HTTP ${status}`);
  }
}

// Only errors that can succeed if sent again (see the table in §3d).
const RETRYABLE_STATUS = new Set([408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504]);

export function isRetryable(err: unknown): boolean {
  if (err instanceof HttpError) {
    return RETRYABLE_STATUS.has(err.status);
  }
  if (err instanceof Error && err.name === 'TimeoutError') {
    return true; // our own AbortSignal.timeout fired
  }
  if (err instanceof TypeError) {
    return true; // fetch's network failure: DNS, refused, reset
  }
  return false; // 4xx and anything unknown: don't retry
}

3. Retry with capped exponential backoff and full jitter

// retry.ts (continued)
type RetryOptions = {
  attempts?: number; // total tries, including the first
  baseMs?: number; // backoff ceiling for the first retry
  capMs?: number; // never wait longer than this
  timeoutMs?: number; // per-attempt timeout
};

const sleep = (ms: number) =>
  new Promise((resolve) => setTimeout(resolve, ms));

export async function withRetry<T>(
  fn: (signal: AbortSignal) => Promise<T>,
  opts: RetryOptions = {},
): Promise<T> {
  const {
    attempts = 3,
    baseMs = 100,
    capMs = 2_000,
    timeoutMs = 800,
  } = opts;

  for (let attempt = 0; ; attempt++) {
    try {
      return await fn(AbortSignal.timeout(timeoutMs));
    } catch (err) {
      const isLastTry = attempt + 1 >= attempts;
      if (isLastTry || !isRetryable(err)) throw err;

      // Full jitter: a random wait between 0 and the capped backoff,
      // so clients that failed together don't retry together.
      const ceiling = Math.min(capMs, baseMs * 2 ** attempt);
      await sleep(Math.random() * ceiling);
    }
  }
}
// users.client.ts: using it in a Nest service
@Injectable()
export class UsersClient {
  constructor(private readonly config: ConfigService) {}

  getUser(id: string) {
    const api = this.config.getOrThrow<string>('USERS_API');

    return withRetry(async (signal) => {
      const res = await fetch(`${api}/users/${id}`, { signal });
      if (!res.ok) throw new HttpError(res.status);
      return res.json();
    });
  }
}

This is a GET, so retrying is safe. For a POST that creates something, don't retry unless the API takes an idempotency key (#2). A 404 throws HttpError(404), which isn't retryable, so it fails at once.

4. A retry budget (the token-bucket idea)

// retry-budget.ts
// Each retry spends 1 token; each success earns back 0.1.
// So retries can't stay above ~10% of successful traffic, even in an outage.
export class RetryBudget {
  private tokens: number;

  constructor(
    private readonly max = 10,
    private readonly earnPerSuccess = 0.1,
  ) {
    this.tokens = max;
  }

  /** Call before retrying. false means the budget is empty: fail now. */
  trySpend(): boolean {
    if (this.tokens < 1) return false;
    this.tokens -= 1;
    return true;
  }

  onSuccess(): void {
    this.tokens = Math.min(this.max, this.tokens + this.earnPerSuccess);
  }
}

Simpler than the article's version: when tokens run out this fails at once, while AWS's keeps retrying at a slow fixed rate. Share one budget per downstream service (a singleton provider in Nest).

5. Deterministic jitter for crons

// reports.job.ts (uses @nestjs/schedule)
import { createHash } from 'node:crypto';
import { hostname } from 'node:os';

// Same pod, same offset (0–5 min) every night. A spike at 00:03:17
// then always points to the same pod.
const offsetMs =
  createHash('sha256').update(hostname()).digest().readUInt32BE(0) %
  300_000;

@Injectable()
export class ReportsJob {
  @Cron('0 0 * * *')
  async nightly() {
    await sleep(offsetMs);
    await this.buildReports();
  }
}

✅ Code-review checklist

  • Does every outbound call (HTTP, DB, Redis, queue) have a timeout covering connect + response?
  • Is there exactly one retrying layer on this call path?
  • Are retries capped (count and total time) and jittered?
  • Is the operation safe to repeat? If not, is there an idempotency key?
  • Do we only retry retryable errors (timeouts, 5xx, 429, network), never 400/401/403?
  • Do scheduled jobs and client reconnects have jitter?

🧪 Lab (25 min): cause a retry storm, then fix it

A tiny server that handles 20 requests at a time and rejects the rest with 503. Then 200 clients start at once. Save as lab.mjs and run node lab.mjs immediate, then backoff, then jitter.

import http from 'node:http';

// A server that can work on 20 requests at a time and rejects the rest.
const CAPACITY = 20;
let inFlight = 0;
let served = 0;
let rejected = 0;

const server = http.createServer((req, res) => {
  if (inFlight >= CAPACITY) {
    rejected++;
    res.writeHead(503).end();
    return;
  }
  inFlight++;
  setTimeout(() => {
    inFlight--;
    served++;
    res.end('ok');
  }, 50); // 50 ms of "work"
});

const sleep = (ms) => new Promise((resolve) => setTimeout(resolve, ms));

// How long to wait before retry number `attempt` (0, 1, 2, ...).
const STRATEGIES = {
  immediate: () => 0,
  backoff: (attempt) => Math.min(2000, 50 * 2 ** attempt),
  jitter: (attempt) => Math.random() * Math.min(2000, 50 * 2 ** attempt),
};

function get(port) {
  return new Promise((resolve) => {
    http
      .get(`http://127.0.0.1:${port}/`, (res) => {
        res.resume();
        resolve(res.statusCode);
      })
      .on('error', () => resolve(0));
  });
}

async function client(port, delayFor) {
  for (let attempt = 0; ; attempt++) {
    const status = await get(port);
    if (status === 200) return;
    await sleep(delayFor(attempt));
  }
}

const name = process.argv[2] ?? 'jitter';

server.listen(0, async () => {
  const { port } = server.address();
  const start = Date.now();
  const clients = Array.from({ length: 200 }, () =>
    client(port, STRATEGIES[name]),
  );
  await Promise.all(clients);

  const calls = served + rejected;
  const ms = Date.now() - start;
  console.log(`${name}: calls=${calls} rejected=${rejected} time=${ms}ms`);
  server.close();
});

🎤 In the interview

① Where it shows up

Almost every design round has a moment like "what if service X is slow or down?". It's central in: payment or checkout systems, webhook / notification delivery, rate limiters (clients receiving 429s), chat apps (reconnect storms after a deploy), distributed job schedulers and any microservice call chain.

② The 30-second version

"Every remote call gets a timeout based on the dependency's p99.9 latency. I retry only transient errors, in one layer, with exponential backoff and full jitter so clients don't sync up, and I cap retries with a budget so an outage doesn't multiply load. Writes get idempotency keys so retries are safe."

③ The 2-minute version (say it out loud)

  1. Why: slow calls hold sockets and memory, so without timeouts one slow dependency takes us down too.
  2. Timeout value: from the dependency's latency percentiles, e.g. p99.9 plus padding, covering connect, TLS and response.
  3. Retries are selfish: fine for blips, harmful in overload. Only 5xx, 429 and timeouts, never 4xx; honor Retry-After.
  4. Where: one layer only, because retries multiply: 3 attempts across 5 layers is up to 243× load.
  5. How: capped exponential backoff with full jitter; a retry budget (token bucket) so retries stay a small share of traffic.
  6. Safety: a timeout doesn't mean it failed, so writes need idempotency keys.
  7. Beyond: propagate deadlines down the chain; during a real outage, fail fast and degrade.

④ SDE1 vs SDE2 answer

Prompt: "Your checkout service calls a payment provider that's sometimes flaky. How do you handle it?"

SDE1 answer
"I'd wrap the call in a retry, maybe 3 attempts, and log errors."
SDE2 answer
"800 ms timeout from their p99.9. Retry only timeouts, 5xx and 429, with exponential backoff and full jitter, max 3 attempts within a 3 s total deadline. Every charge carries an idempotency key, so a retry after a timeout can't double-charge. Retries live only in the payment client, with a retry budget. If the provider is down, I stop retrying, mark the order pending, and reconcile from a queue. I alert on retry rate, not just errors."

The difference isn't vocabulary. It's numbers, safety (idempotency), where retries live, and what happens when it's really down.

⑤ Likely follow-ups

"How did you pick that timeout?"

From the dependency's latency distribution: pick an acceptable false-timeout rate (0.1%) and use the matching percentile (p99.9), plus padding if the distribution is tight, plus network latency for far-away clients.

"What if the provider is completely down for 10 minutes?"

Retries won't help, so the retry budget runs dry and we fail fast. Degrade gracefully: accept the order as "payment pending", queue it, and process when the provider is healthy. Alert. Optionally a circuit breaker, knowing it's a blunter tool.

"Why jitter if you already have exponential backoff?"

Clients that failed together back off by the same amounts and retry together, so the spike comes back. With a cap they all retry at the same fixed rate. Jitter decorrelates them; in Brooker's simulation with 100 clients it cut the call count by more than half.

"Five services in the chain each retry. Problem?"

Yes: retries multiply, up to attempts^layers (3⁵ = 243×). Retry in one layer, ideally where you know the operation is idempotent, and propagate a deadline so lower layers stop early.

"The server is recovering and 50k mobile clients reconnect. What happens?"

A thundering herd knocks it down again. Clients need jittered backoff on reconnect, and the server needs load shedding / admission control because we don't control old app versions.

⑥ Red flags that cost points

  • "Just retry 3 times", with no backoff, jitter or cap.
  • Retrying every error, including 400/401/403.
  • Retrying POSTs without mentioning idempotency.
  • No timeouts, or "set it to 30 seconds to be safe".
  • Not asking what happens when the dependency is fully down.

⑦ Numbers worth knowing

  • 3⁵ = 243×: 5 layers × 3 attempts each (from the article).
  • 0.1% false timeouts ↔ p99.9: the article's example for picking a timeout.
  • ~20 ms timeout broke on fresh TLS connections after deploys (the article's story).
  • >50% fewer calls with jitter at 100 contending clients (Brooker's 2015 simulation).

⚠️ Traps

  • "The library retries for me, so I'll add my own retry too." → Now 2 layers multiply. Check what your SDK or client already does.
  • Timeout on the request but not on getting a pool connection. → Requests queue forever waiting for a DB slot. Time out both.
  • Retrying non-idempotent POSTs on timeout. → A timeout doesn't mean it failed. The order may exist; now it exists twice.
  • Backoff without jitter. → You've only moved the stampede a few seconds later (the lab's 9-second row).
  • Retrying forever "because it's important". → That's how a 1-minute blip becomes a 1-hour outage.

✅ Check yourself

1. Why does the article call retries "selfish"?

A retry spends the server's capacity to raise your chance of success. Fine when failures are rare; during overload, everyone's selfish retries add up to more load on a server that's already drowning.

2. API → service → DB layer → driver → DB, and each layer tries 3 times. Worst case?

3⁴ = 81 calls to the database for one user action (243 with five retrying layers, as in the article). Retry in one layer.

3. How would you choose a timeout for an internal pricing service?

Its latency percentiles: pick a false-timeout rate (0.1%), take that percentile (p99.9), pad it if p99.9 is close to p50, and make sure it covers connect, TLS and response.

4. Your nightly cron runs on 20 pods and DB CPU spikes at 00:00. Fix?

A deterministic per-pod offset (hash of the host name, modulo a few minutes), or run the job on one leader. Deterministic beats random so the pattern is debuggable.

⏱️ 5. Mini design (10 min): webhook delivery for an e-commerce platform. Merchants' endpoints are often slow or down. Sketch it, then compare.
  • Durable queue of deliveries; workers pull from it, so a slow merchant never blocks the order flow.
  • Timeout per delivery (seconds, not minutes) so one slow endpoint doesn't hold a worker.
  • Retry schedule: exponential backoff with jitter, capped, stretching over hours for long outages; then a dead-letter queue plus a "replay" button and an alert to the merchant.
  • Idempotency: each event carries a unique id header so the merchant can drop duplicates, because a timeout may have been delivered.
  • Isolation: a per-merchant concurrency limit or budget, so one dead endpoint can't eat all workers.
  • Jitter on recovery: when a merchant comes back, the backlog drains at a controlled rate, not all at once.
  • Metrics: success rate, retry rate and backlog age per merchant.

Numbers like "seconds" and "hours" here are design choices to justify in the room, not facts from the article.

📝 Remember card

  • ⏱️ Every remote call gets a timeout, and it covers DNS, connect, TLS and response.
  • 📊 Timeout ≈ downstream p99.9, plus padding when latency is tight.
  • 🔁 Retry rarely, in one layer, only retryable errors on safe operations.
  • 📈 Exponential backoff + cap + max attempts + full jitter.
  • 🪣 Budget retries as a share of traffic (token bucket).
  • 🎲 Jitter anything synchronized: crons, reconnects, cache TTLs. Deterministic jitter for scheduled work.
  • 🚨 During an outage, fail fast and degrade; don't retry harder.

🃏 Flashcards

How do you pick a timeout value?

Downstream's latency percentile matching your acceptable false-timeout rate (0.1% → p99.9), plus padding and network latency.

Why add jitter on top of exponential backoff?

Without it, clients that failed together retry together, recreating the spike.

Why retry in only one layer?

Retries multiply: attempts^layers (3⁵ = 243×).

What makes a retry of a write safe?

Idempotency, e.g. an idempotency key the server uses to drop duplicates.

Amazon's alternative to circuit breakers?

A local token bucket for retries: retry freely while tokens last, then at a fixed low rate.
